Stock Performance After IPO

How US IPOs from 2022 through 2026 have performed relative to their offer prices — winners, losers, how often and when IPO stocks fall, and the factors that predict it.

Data as of June 10–11, 2026 · prices are intraday snapshots

The headline

Most IPOs from this cycle are underwater. Only 38.3% of 2025 IPOs were trading above their offer price at year-end 2025, and several large names have fallen further since. Gains are heavily concentrated in AI infrastructure and stablecoins; losses cluster in fintech/BNPL, crypto, and LNG.

Earlier cohorts: 2022–2024

The post-2021 bust years were tiny — 2022 saw just 71 IPOs raising $7.7B (vs 397 raising $142.4B in 2021), recovering to 108 IPOs / $19.4B in 2023 and 150 / $29.6B in 2024. With more time in the market, these cohorts show the classic IPO pattern: a few enormous winners, a money-losing median. Per Ritter's data (through 12/31/25), the average 2022 IPO's 3-year buy-and-hold return was −29.5% (−72.2% market-adjusted) and 2023's was −30.9% (−88.3% market-adjusted) — the worst market-adjusted cohorts in decades, because the S&P rallied while small IPOs collapsed. When the declines happen

The underperformance is back-loaded, and the verified numbers give a rough timetable. Versus size-matched firms (1980–2024), IPOs lag by only −0.6% in the first six months, then −5.5% in months 7–12 and −7.9% in year two, fading to −3.8% in year three.

Can you time it? Not precisely — these are averages, not guarantees for any one stock. But the shape is consistent: the seller's moment is the first-day pop; the buyer's risk concentrates in months 7–24; and a stock that survives to year three with the business intact has typically stopped behaving like "an IPO." Watch the lockup date (in the S-1), the first two earnings reports, and whether insiders actually sell when they can.

Takeaways

  • The pop fades. Big first-day gains (Figma's +250% day one was a record for a billion-dollar IPO) say little about where a stock trades a year later.
  • Theme concentration is extreme. AI infrastructure (CoreWeave, Cerebras) and stablecoins (Circle) account for nearly all the large-cap winners across both cohorts.
  • Fintech and crypto listings have been punishing — Klarna −59%, Chime −37%, eToro −33%, Gemini −61%, BitGo −70%.
  • The index lagged badly. An investor holding the IPO cohort broadly (Renaissance IPO Index) earned +5% in 2025 vs +18% for the S&P 500, then lost −8% in Q1 2026.

What makes IPO stocks go up or down?

Decades of academic research — most of it from Jay Ritter's IPO dataset (1980–2024, 9,253 IPOs) — shows the average IPO underperforms the market by about 3.6% per year for five years after listing. But the averages hide enormous spreads between cohorts. These are the factors with the strongest evidence.

Does founder ownership predict better returns?

The evidence leans yes, with caveats. Founder-CEO firms among large US companies earned +8.3%/year benchmark-adjusted over 1993–2002 (+4.4%/year after controlling for firm, CEO, and industry characteristics — so it is not purely a tech-sector artifact). Dual-class IPOs, the classic founder-control structure, returned +29.5% over 3 years vs +18.0% for single-class IPOs; among tech IPOs, dual-class beat the market by 13.8% while single-class lagged by 15.4%.

The catch: dual-class firms command a valuation premium at the IPO that dissipates as the firm matures — founder control looks like an early-life-cycle advantage, not a permanent one. The effect is also weaker than profitability or scale, and institutional studies report mixed results for mature dual-class firms. Read it as a mild positive signal, strongest in tech and in the first years after listing.

General drivers of any stock's direction

  • Earnings surprises — the strongest short-horizon driver; stocks drift in the direction of the surprise for weeks (post-earnings-announcement drift). Well-established.
  • Interest rates & macro — rising real rates compress valuations, hitting long-duration growth stocks (like recent IPOs) hardest. Well-established mechanism.
  • Momentum — 6–12 month winners keep winning on average; among the most replicated anomalies, but prone to violent crashes.
  • Float & short constraints — small floats with divergent opinions produce overpricing that corrects as float grows; high short interest predicts underperformance.
  • Dilution vs buybacks — net share issuers underperform net repurchasers; robust cross-sectional finding.
  • Index inclusion — one-time positive pop on S&P 500 addition; the effect has shrunk over time. Weak/diminishing.
  • Institutional ownership — rising ownership accompanies outperformance, but causality is murky. Mixed.
Bottom line: the most powerful predictors for a post-IPO stock are scale and profitability at listing, the valuation multiple, the era (hot vs cold IPO market), underwriter tier, VC backing, and founder/dual-class structure — then ongoing earnings surprises and the rate environment once the stock is seasoned. Founder ownership helps, but it is a weaker signal than "was it profitable and reasonably priced when it listed." This maps onto the 2025–26 cohorts above: Klarna and Chime (unprofitable fintechs at rich multiples) fit the loser profile; CoreWeave and Cerebras (large scale, real revenue) fit the survivors.

Methodology & caveats

  • All "current" prices are intraday snapshots from June 10, 2026 and will drift; exact percentages may vary by a few points depending on quote timing.
  • The 202-IPO count (Renaissance Capital) covers deals with ≥$50M market cap and excludes SPACs and closed-end funds; the 347-listing count (stockanalysis.com) includes SPACs and micro-caps. The two methodologies should not be mixed.
  • The 38.3%-above-offer figure and the "other notable 2025 names" returns are from Renaissance's mid/late-December 2025 snapshot, not June 2026. Given that Figma and Bullish have since flipped below offer, the share above offer today is likely lower.
  • No verified June-2026 above/below-offer split exists for the full cohorts; a claim attempting one for recent 2026 deals failed independent verification and is excluded.
  • In the factors section, figures marked "well-established"/"established" were verified against the cited primary studies; items marked "canonical" are standard findings from the IPO literature (Ritter 1991 and Ritter's data files) reported as published. Academic results are long-period averages and may not hold in any given year or for any given stock.
  • 2022–2024 individual prices are from stockanalysis.com as of June 11, 2026; cohort counts are Renaissance Capital's (≥$50M market cap, ex-SPACs). Ritter's cohort return averages (2022: −29.5% 3-yr; 2023: −30.9%) use his stricter operating-company definition (38, 54, and 72 IPOs in 2022–24) with returns through Dec 31, 2025 — the huge market-adjusted shortfalls partly reflect the strong S&P rally over the same window.
  • The fall-frequency statistics (56.1% / 57.1% below offer at 3 and 5 years) cover 1975–2021 IPOs and are measured from the offer price; measured from the first-day close the medians are worse (−25.7% at 3 years, −32.0% at 5).
